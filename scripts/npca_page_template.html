Is AI progress accelerating? It depends on the shape of each benchmark’s curve

Epoch's Capabilities Index assumes every benchmark score follows one S-shaped curve of capability: sigmoid(slope × (capability − difficulty)). That is a choice. Below, eight different curves are fitted to each benchmark, from ECI's logistic to curves of any monotone shape, and compared on scores they never saw. Then each is fitted jointly with capability, to see how it changes the measured speed and acceleration of frontier progress. The curves roughly agree on the rate up to a year ago (12–15 ECI points a year). The curves that predict newer models best measure faster recent growth than ECI, but how much faster is not pinned down by the data.

Fit

The frontier, measured on each benchmark's own curve

The thick line is frontier θ (running best by release date, from GPT-4 on). Each thin line reads the same frontier models off one benchmark's free curve and converts back to capability as if that curve were a sigmoid: midpoint + log-odds ÷ slope. If ECI's sigmoid were exactly right, every thin line would lie on the thick one. Where they fan out, the measured rate of progress depends on which benchmark's scale you trust. Lines are drawn only where the frontier is inside a benchmark's scored range and the curve is between 2% and 98%, since inverting a saturated curve is meaningless. The curve switch in the first section applies here too.

Method

Model. scoremb = gb(um · db), where um is a model's position (one or two coordinates), db = (cos αb, sin αb) a benchmark's direction (|α| ≤ 70°, softly shrunk towards 0), and gb a nondecreasing curve. A sigmoid gb with one factor is ECI. A monotone gb is piecewise linear on knots with nonnegative rises, fit by nonnegative least squares. It is penalised for changes in slope and shrunk towards that benchmark's own best sigmoid, so a benchmark with few scores stays close to the ECI shape and one with many can bend.

Fitting. Same objective as Epoch's: squared error in score space over the 2,844 scored cells, plus penalties. The curve penalties (smoothness , shrinkage ) and the two-factor priors (axis-2 sd, angle sd) were chosen by 5-fold CV on the same folds reported above. That makes the tuned fits' random-CV errors slightly optimistic, but the forward test was not used for tuning. Fitting alternates three steps until the error stops falling: refit each curve; for two factors, choose each benchmark's angle by grid search; place each model by coarse-to-fine grid search, with a weak prior. Positions are re-anchored after each pass so Claude 3.5 Sonnet = 130 and GPT-5 = 150 on axis 1. Every fit, including those inside cross-validation, starts from Epoch's ECI refit on that fit's own training scores (scripts/eci_fit.py), so no held-out score enters through the starting point. The one-factor sigmoid fit reproduces Epoch's in-sample error to within 0.3%.

What is identified. With a free monotone curve and one factor, any monotone stretch of the capability axis can be absorbed into the curves. The data then pin down only the order of models, not the distances between them. Here the spacing is inherited from the ECI start and the anchors. Claims about the speed of progress therefore rest on the sigmoid assumption. The last chart shows how much they would change if each benchmark's own curve defined the scale. With two factors, the requirement that every direction's projection works through one curve constrains the plane much more tightly.

Uncertainty. Bands on the curves are the 10th–90th percentiles over refits on models resampled with replacement (the two anchor models always kept). Intervals on held-out error come from resampling models.

Not done here. A Gaussian-copula (rank-likelihood) factor model would give a fully Bayesian version and handle tied 0/1 scores formally. Three or more factors are not fit: with a median of 9 scores per model, two is already a lot to ask.
